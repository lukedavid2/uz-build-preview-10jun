Rhythm & comping — the 90% job

On a bandstand you will spend nine bars accompanying for every bar you solo — and the players who get called back are the ones who make those nine bars feel good. This chapter is the groove curriculum most lesson sites bury behind the lead material: the shuffle, the boogie pattern, the chord vocabulary, the slow blues, and the discipline of playing behind a singer.

The shuffle — the engine

Blues eighth notes are not straight. Each beat splits into a triplet, and you play the first and last note of it — long-short, long-short. Count "doo-dah, doo-dah". The silent middle triplet is where the swing lives:

Practise with a metronome clicking triplets before you ever practise with a backing track. The two classic failures: playing straight eighths where a shuffle belongs, and letting the "dah" drift late. Record one minute of your shuffle and listen back — the tape never lies.

The boogie pattern — your first thousand gigs

The two-note pattern under half the blues ever recorded: hold the root's power chord, rock the top note from the 5th up to the 6th (and back), shuffled. Move the same shape to the IV and V and you can play an entire 12-bar:

Variations to add once it's automatic: walk up to the ♭7 (5–6–♭7–6), palm-mute everything, and accent beats 2 and 4 with the band's snare. Jimmy Reed built a career here; so did early ZZ Top.

The uptown vocabulary: 7ths and 9ths

Past the boogie, blues comping runs on small movable chords — not big barres. The hierarchy: dominant 7th shapes for the meat-and-potatoes sound, 9th chords for the uptown T-Bone/B.B. flavour, and 13ths when you want the horn-section shimmer. The trick that makes all of them sound professional: approach from a half-step — slide into A9 from B♭9 or G♯9 on beat one. One slide, instant authority.

  • Three-note grips beat six-note grips. Root–3rd–♭7, or just 3rd and ♭7 if a bassist is present. Small chords leave room for the singer and cut through the band.
  • The Freddie Green move: hit a clipped chord on all four beats, barely letting it ring. Instant swing era.
  • The Jimmy Reed high end: two-string stabs on the top strings, answering the vocal. Comping is call-and-response too.

The slow blues — 12/8, the jam-night inevitability

Every jam eventually calls a slow blues, and it exposes everyone whose time depends on tempo. Slow blues runs in 12/8: four big beats, each carrying a full triplet. Count it like this:

Comping a slow blues: less is everything. Hold chords and swell; place the 9th-chord stab on beat one and get out of the way; save the busy triplet fills for the last bar of a phrase (the band's collective "amen"). When the singer breathes, you may answer — one short fill, low register, then silence. Texas slow blues (think Texas Flood) lives or dies on this discipline.

Dynamics: the band is a singer

The cheapest trick that separates real bands from jam-night chaos: play the verse quieter than the chorus, and the first chorus quieter than the last. Drop to half-volume the moment the vocal enters. Build each 12 bars one notch. The blues is dynamic theatre — the chords are too simple to carry a flat performance.

Tie-in: loop a 12-bar in the builder, set the style and try comping against it — stabs on beat one only, then Freddie Green fours, then Jimmy Reed answers. Three different bands, same loop.
