Blues soloing — phrasing first, scales second

Nobody ever moved a room with a scale. Blues soloing is a language of two-bar sentences, bent notes that live between the piano keys, and the discipline to leave space. Here is the vocabulary in the order it actually matters — drawn out on the neck.

1. Call and response — the sentence structure

The solo inherits the AAB shape of the lyric. Play a short phrase (the call), let it breathe, then answer — by repeating it with one note changed, or resolving it somewhere new. A 12-bar chorus comfortably holds three sentences. Listen to B.B. King on Live at the Regal: he sings a line, Lucille answers. That conversation is the whole art.

Exercise: one phrase per two bars, five notes maximum, for an entire chorus. Boring? Then make the rhythm of each phrase more interesting — that is the lesson.

2. Home base: minor pentatonic box 1

Five notes, one position, and the two blue notes marked. The ♭3 wants to be curled sharp — bend it a quarter-tone toward the major 3rd. The ♭5 is seasoning to pass through, never to sit on:

3. The blend: borrowing the major colours

Minor pentatonic is grit; major pentatonic (2, 3 and 6 instead of ♭3, 4 and ♭7) is sweetness. The masters pivot constantly — the working rule while your ear develops: major-lean on the I chord, minor-lean on the IV and V. Here are the major colours sitting inside the same hand position:

4. The B.B. box — one position, infinite mileage

B.B. King spent a career inside a four-fret box around the root on the B string. The 2nd sits ready to bend up to the 3rd, the 6th and ♭7 a finger apart — sweet and sour side by side. Learn it in A, then move it. The box teaches the central truth: where you can sing, you can play:

5. Linking positions (when one box gets small)

The minor pentatonic has five overlapping boxes up the neck; you don't need them all at once. The 80/20: learn box 2 (it starts where box 1's top finger ends) and slide between the two on the B and G strings. That diagonal — box 1, slide, box 2, B.B. box — covers nearly everything the masters play below the 12th fret.

6. Bends and vibrato — the voice

  • Bend with three fingers behind the bending finger; bend to a target pitch, not a distance. Check every bend against the fretted note it's aiming at.
  • The curl: the ♭3 pushed a quarter-tone sharp — the most blues-sounding sound there is.
  • Albert's move: a full-tone bend that keeps travelling — 1 into 2, kissing ♭3 — then releases late.
  • Vibrato: develop it on one long note a day. B.B.'s butterfly is fast and narrow; Albert's moan is slow and wide. Yours should be a choice, not a tremor.

7. Fills — soloing's day job

Between vocal lines you get roughly two beats to comment. The craft: answer the melody, don't compete with it. Stay out of the singer's register, start your fill as the vocal phrase ends (not before), and finish on a chord tone as the next line starts. One fill per vocal gap, and if another instrument filled, you don't. At a jam this single discipline marks you as a professional faster than any solo.

8. Following the changes (or deliberately not)

Two honest approaches. Down-home: one scale over the whole form, the band's harmony supplies the tension. Uptown (T-Bone onward): target chord tones — land the 3rd of each chord as it arrives (C♯ over A7, F♯ over D7, G♯ over E7). Practise uptown even if you prefer down-home: knowing where the 3rds live is what makes one-scale phrasing land on purpose instead of by luck.
