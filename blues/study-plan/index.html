Study plan & jam survival — a two-week on-ramp

You can noodle at the blues for years or own its skeleton in a fortnight. The difference is sequencing: form first, feel second, vocabulary third. Below is the whole fortnight at a glance, then a real lesson for every day — what you’re training, the exercises, and how to know you got it — plus the first ten songs to learn and a field guide to your first jam night.

The daily shape — 25 minutes, same skeleton every day
  1. 5 min warm-up: yesterday’s material, half speed, no judgement.
  2. 15 min the day’s work: the exercises below, metronome or builder loop running.
  3. 5 min play: just jam over the loop with whatever you have. This is where it becomes music, not homework.

Week one — form and feel

Day 1 — Count it

Today the 12-bar stops being a diagram and becomes a place you can stand. You are training one thing: always knowing which bar you’re in.

  • Loop a basic 12-bar in A in the builder at 80 bpm. Count aloud — "ONE-2-3-4, TWO-2-3-4…" — for ten full choruses. Boring on purpose; boredom means it’s working.
  • Now clap only beat 1 of every bar for three choruses. Then clap only bar 9’s beat 1 — the V chord — for five choruses. Feeling bar 9 approach is the whole skill.
  • Mute the strings with your fretting hand and strum the form’s rhythm — no chords, just time. One chorus of pure groove.

You got it when: someone pauses the loop mid-chorus and you can say which bar you’re in without counting back.

Day 2 — Call it

Yesterday you followed the form; today you predict it. Calling changes before they arrive is what separates players who know the form from players who chase it.

  • Loop the 12-bar; shout "FOUR!" on the last beat of bar 4 and "FIVE!" on the last beat of bar 8 — one beat before each change. Ten choruses.
  • Play only root notes on beat 1 of each bar (A, D, E on the open strings), eyes closed, five choruses. Wrong root = start the chorus again.
  • Sing the root movement as the loop plays — low "doo" for I, up for IV, higher for V. The ear leads the hands forever after.

You got it when: ten eyes-closed choruses of roots with zero wrong landings.

Day 3 — The quick change

One chord different — bar 2 jumps to the IV. Today is spot-the-difference ear training, because at a jam nobody will show you; they’ll just say "quick change in A".

  • Alternate choruses in the builder: basic, quick change, basic, quick change. Call out "home" or "four" as you hit bar 2.
  • Listening drill: play Sweet Home Chicago▶, then Pride and Joy▶, then Hide Away▶. In each, catch what bar 2 does within the first chorus. Rewind, confirm.
  • Roots-only drill from Day 2, but on the quick change. Eyes closed, five choruses.

You got it when: four bars into any recording, you can say whether it quick-changed.

Day 4 — The shuffle

The feel that makes it blues. Each beat is a triplet; you play the first and last note — "doo-dah". Work the rhythm chapter alongside today.

  • Metronome clicking triplets at 60 bpm. Say "doo-dah, doo-dah" out loud for two minutes before touching the guitar — the mouth learns faster than the hands.
  • Shuffle on one chord (A7) for five minutes. No changes, no fills; just long-short, long-short, locked to the click.
  • Accent beats 2 and 4 (where the snare lives). Two minutes; feel how it suddenly sounds like a record.

You got it when: you can drop the metronome for one chorus and re-enter still in time with it.

Day 5 — The boogie pattern

The two-note engine under half of recorded blues: root’s power chord, top note rocking 5–6–5, shuffled. Today it goes from your hands into your autopilot.

  • Boogie on the I only (A), 60 bpm, three minutes. Watch the right hand: the shuffle from Day 4 must survive the new left-hand job.
  • Move the same shape to D (IV) and E (V). Two minutes each.
  • Full 12-bar with the boogie, 60 bpm then 80 bpm. The changes happen without looking down.

You got it when: one full chorus, eyes closed, no dropped "dah"s.

Day 6 — Record and face it

The tape never lies, and pros listen back constantly. Today you find your actual weaknesses instead of guessing.

  • Record three choruses of shuffle + boogie on your phone, with the loop or metronome audible.
  • Listen with three questions: Does the "dah" rush? Does any change arrive late? Does chorus three swing as hard as chorus one?
  • Fix the single worst thing — just that one — and re-record once. Keep both takes; in a week the difference will make your day.

You got it when: take two fixes the thing you named, and you can hear it.

Day 7 — Turnaround 1: the descending sixths

The turnaround chapter has the method box and the tab — today is move one, the "Robert Johnson". Use the four-step method: sing it, one key half-speed, then A–E–G, then stress-test.

  • Sing the descending line over bars 11–12 of the loop until it’s hummable cold.
  • In A at 60 bpm: the V7 lands exactly on beat four, ten clean repetitions.
  • Move it to E, then G. Five clean reps each, chord names said aloud.

You got it when: three keys, no rushing the last beat — record bar 12 and check.

Week two — vocabulary and a song of your own

Day 8 — Turnaround 2: the walk-down and walk-up

  • The walk-down (triplets to the 3rd): A at 60, then 80, then in E and G. The four-step method again.
  • The walk-up into the IV (1–2–♭3–3) — pair it with the quick change for a double dose of bar-2 awareness.
  • Alternate moves chorus by chorus over the loop without stopping: sixths, walk-down, sixths, walk-up.

You got it when: you can swap turnarounds mid-loop on a shouted cue.

Day 9 — The ending and the intro

Endings forgive everything that came before; intros buy you confidence on a bandstand.

  • The ♭II9–I9 slide: grab the 9th shape, land it from a half-step above, let it ring. Ten times in A, five in E and G.
  • The "from the five" intro: play bars 9–12 as a four-bar intro, then a full chorus. Three times.
  • Put it together: intro → two choruses (boogie) → turnaround each chorus → ♭II9–I9 ending. Record it. That’s a complete performance.

You got it when: the recording sounds like it knows where it’s going — front door, rooms, back door.

Day 10 — Steal phrase 1

Vocabulary day. Pick one player from the list whose sound you envy and take a phrase off the record by ear — the soloing chapter’s sing-first rule applies.

  • Find a two-bar phrase you love (solo openings are easiest). Loop those ten seconds.
  • Sing it until you can sing it away from the recording. Only then find it on the neck.
  • Play it over your loop in the original spot (bars 1–2 of a chorus), ten times. Then try it starting at bar 5.

You got it when: the phrase survives the move to bar 5 without falling apart.

Day 11 — Steal phrase 2

  • Second phrase, different player, different register (if phrase 1 was low, go high — the B.B. box area).
  • Same routine: loop, sing, find, place. Then play phrase 1 as the call and phrase 2 as the response.
  • Transpose your favourite of the two into E. (Painful, valuable, gets easier forever.)

You got it when: call-and-response with the two phrases sounds like a conversation, not a medley.

Day 12 — Build a chorus

Assembly day: one 12-bar chorus that is yours, built from stolen bricks.

  • Bars 1–4: phrase 1 (call) + your own answer. Bars 5–8: phrase 2 over the IV, answer over the I. Bars 9–12: your biggest moment at bar 9, then your Day-7 turnaround.
  • Play it over the loop until the seams disappear — ten or fifteen passes.
  • Record it twice: once playing safe, once pushing the dynamics (whisper bars 1–8, shout bar 9). Keep the brave take.

You got it when: a listener couldn’t tell which two bars were stolen.

Day 13 — Write: the verses

AAB lyric over your loop: one line, the same line again, an answering punchline. Three verses. Draft against the loop with RhymeForge open — blues rhymes run in couplets and love a good slant.

  • Write verse 1 about something true and small (the bus, the rain, the phone that doesn’t ring). The blues is concrete.
  • Sing it over the loop as you write — lines that don’t fit bar 9’s peak get cut or moved.
  • Verses 2 and 3 escalate: same complaint, higher stakes.

You got it when: the B line of each verse lands on bar 9 like it was born there.

Day 14 — Write: finish it

  • Arrange: intro from the five → verse → verse (add the boogie) → stop-time verse (band stabs beat 1, you sing the gaps — steal the move from Hoochie Coochie Man▶) → final verse, biggest dynamics → ♭II9–I9 ending.
  • Record the whole thing against the loop. Two takes max; perfection is next month’s problem.
  • Save the progression in the builder (File → Save) and export the MIDI if you want to develop it later.

You got it when: you have a recorded blues with your name on it. It won’t be original — that’s fine and traditional. You installed the load-bearing wall; the original house comes later.

Jam survival — a field guide

Before you go

  • Know three ladder songs cold — 1, 3 and 5 cover a shuffle, a Texas groove and a minor blues; that’s 90% of what gets called.
  • One turnaround and one ending, automatic. Nobody hears your scales; everybody hears bar 12.
  • Pack: tuner, two cables, picks, earplugs. Tune before you’re called up.
  • Rehearse the sentence: "Quick change in A, medium shuffle, watch me for the ending." That sentence is the whole interview.

When you arrive

  • Watch one full round before you sign up. Clock who leads, how they count tunes in, and what the house signals are.
  • Introduce yourself to the host; say what you play and that it’s your first time. Hosts protect debutants who ask.

On the bandstand — the etiquette

  • Comp small. Two- and three-note chords, off the singer’s register, quieter than you think. Nobody ever got uninvited for being too tasteful.
  • One fill per gap — and only if nobody else took it. Two players filling at once is the universal sound of an amateur jam.
  • Solo length: one chorus on a slow blues, two on a medium, never more without being waved on. Leave them wanting.
  • Dynamics are communal: when the singer drops, everyone drops. The band is one instrument and the singer is its melody.
  • Eye contact is the protocol. Look up between phrases; the music is steered with faces.

The signals decoder

You seeIt means
Guitar neck raised highLast chorus — head for the ending
Finger circled in the airOne more time around the form
Leader points at youYour solo — start this chorus, not mid-form
Fist raised / hand sliceStop-time or full band break on beat 1
Leader watching the bassist at bar 11Ending now — land the ♭II9–I9 with them

When it goes wrong

  • Lost in the form? Drop to roots on beat 1 and listen for the V–IV descent — bars 9–10 are the form’s landmark. Rejoin at the top.
  • Wrong key? Small chords, no roots, until your ear finds it. A 3rd-and-7th shell in the wrong key is survivable; a barre chord isn’t.
  • Whatever happens, don’t stop playing time. A wrong note in time is jazz; a right note out of time is a trainwreck.
Before you move on, you should be able to…
  • ☐ Call a tune in one sentence: key, form, feel, ending cue.
  • ☐ Comp three full choruses without playing a single fill.
  • ☐ Name the five bandstand signals without looking.
  • ☐ Recover from a lost-form moment using the bars 9–10 landmark.

Month two — depth

The fortnight installed the skeleton; month two grows muscle. One theme per week, same daily 25 minutes, workouts moving to intermediate tier:

Week 3 — the slow blues

  • The 12/8 chapter daily: count it, comp it with swells, one fill per phrase maximum. Loop a slow minor blues at 50 bpm — slower than feels reasonable.
  • Study Red House▶: one verse of comping copied exactly, one solo chorus transcribed phrase by phrase across the week.
  • Record two minutes of slow-blues comping. The audit question: could a singer live inside it?

Week 4 — the keys tour

  • Everything you own — form, boogie, two turnarounds, box 1 — in E and G (jam keys), then B♭ (horn key, harder, worth it).
  • The soloing workout’s "target the 3rd" drill in each new key; the 3rds move, the logic doesn’t.
  • End of week: a full performance (intro → three choruses → ending) in a key you couldn’t play on Monday.

Week 5 — dynamics and band-craft

  • The feel-switching drill from the rhythm workout daily: shuffle → straight → rhumba → 12/8 as one performance.
  • Comp against your own recorded voice (sing, hum, talk twelve bars). Mix check: voice always wins.
  • Dynamics map: play the same chorus at four volumes on cue. Loud is easy; quiet-but-intense is the skill.

Week 6 — repertoire push and the first jam

  • Songs 4–7 of the ladder by the full method (form → feel → vocabulary → memory).
  • Re-read the field guide above; rehearse your one-sentence tune call out loud. Then go. Play two tunes, comp well, land the endings, leave them wanting.
  • Debrief in writing within a day: what collapsed under pressure? That list is month three’s syllabus.

Month three — vocabulary and a voice

Weeks 7–8 — the transcription quota

  • One phrase per week minimum, by ear, from the players list — different player each week. Sing, find, copy articulation, move to three keys.
  • Re-phrase each steal three ways (behind the beat, doubled, as a question) — the advanced soloing workout. Stolen and digested beats stolen and framed.
  • Add the B.B. box and the major/minor blend to your map; one chorus daily connecting all three positions.

Week 9 — write the second blues

  • Different form than your first: minor, rhumba feel, or 8-bar. Arrange it fully — intro, stop-time, dynamics arc, ending.
  • Lyric discipline: concrete nouns, escalating verses, the punchline landing bar 9. RhymeForge for the couplets.

Week 10 — the demo and the second jam

  • Record both your blues plus one ladder song, two takes each, phone is fine. Pick the keepers; write one sentence on what the third take would fix.
  • Second jam night. This time: call a tune, take two choruses, cue the ending yourself.

The advanced routine — ongoing, 45 minutes × 4 per week

Past month three the blues becomes maintenance plus stretch. The architecture below keeps all four muscles loaded; the monthly milestones stop it going stale:

MinutesBlockWhat happens
10EarTranscribe (continue the weekly phrase) or sing-and-find: intervals, turnaround lines, bass walks
10FeelOne groove from the map at three tempos, or feel-switching against a drum loop; record fortnightly and compare
10VocabularyThis week’s phrase through three keys and three registers; one constraint chorus (three notes, one string, 50% rest)
10RepertoireOne ladder song polished or one new song; revision rotation so nothing decays
5FreeRecord, play anything, no judgement — then one honest listen-back note
  • Monthly milestones: lead three tunes at a jam including one slow blues; play a chorus that a non-musician friend can hum back; comp for 30 minutes without your right hand stiffening; play your strongest turnaround in all twelve keys.
  • Quarterly: re-record the Day-14 song and file it next to the original. The distance between takes is the only progress bar that matters.

Listening spine (chronological)

  1. Bessie Smith — St. Louis Blues (1925) — phrasing source code
  2. Robert Johnson — King of the Delta Blues Singers — the Delta codex: turnarounds, time, menace
  3. Muddy Waters — The Best of Muddy Waters — the Delta goes electric; the riff as architecture
  4. Howlin' Wolf — Moanin' in the Moonlight — rhythm-section violence, Sumlin’s angles
  5. T-Bone Walker — T-Bone Blues — the birth of uptown electric soloing
  6. B.B. King — Live at the Regal — call-and-response, the box, the audience as instrument
  7. Albert King — Born Under a Bad Sign — bending as a complete language
  8. Magic Sam — West Side Soul — the modern small-band blueprint
  9. Stevie Ray Vaughan — Texas Flood — the synthesis, plus the hardest shuffle on record
