Study plan & jam survival — a two-week on-ramp

You can noodle at the blues for years or own its skeleton in a fortnight. The difference is sequencing: form first, feel second, vocabulary third. Here is the whole fortnight at a glance, then the details — plus the first ten songs to learn and how to survive your first jam night.

Days 1–3: get the form into your body

  • Loop a basic 12-bar in A in the builder. Count "1-2-3-4, 2-2-3-4…" aloud through every bar. Boring on purpose.
  • Graduate to calling changes before they arrive: say "FOUR!" on bar 4's last beat, "FIVE!" on bar 8's. Eyes closed and correct = the form is in.
  • Repeat with the quick change. Spot-the-difference is the ear training.

Days 4–6: the shuffle and the boogie

Work the rhythm chapter: shuffle drill against a triplet metronome, then the boogie pattern on I–IV–V until the changes happen without looking. Day 6 is recording day — one minute of your shuffle, listened back honestly.

Days 7–9: three turnarounds

From the turnaround chapter: descending sixths, the walk-down, and the ♭II9–I9 ending. One per day, three keys each (A, E, G). The highest-value 24 bars of practice in blues guitar.

Days 10–12: one chorus of vocabulary

Pick one player from the list. Lift two phrases by ear. Build one 12-bar chorus using both phrases as call-and-response with your own connecting material. Play it over the loop until it sounds inevitable.

Days 13–14: write one

Write a blues. AAB lyric, three verses, one stop-time section, your turnaround, done. It won't be original — that's fine and traditional. You're installing the load-bearing wall; the original house comes later. Draft the lyric against the loop with RhymeForge open: blues rhymes run in couplets and love a good slant.

Jam survival — the unwritten rules

  • Before you plug in: know the key, the form (12-bar? quick change? 8-bar?) and the feel (shuffle? straight? slow 12/8?). It's one sentence: "Quick change in A, medium shuffle."
  • Eye contact is the protocol. Watch the leader for solo handoffs and the ending signal (a raised neck, a circled finger = last chorus).
  • Comp small. Two-note chords, off the singer's register, quieter than you think. Nobody ever got uninvited for being too tasteful.
  • One fill per gap — and only if nobody else took it. Two players filling at once is the universal sound of an amateur jam.
  • Solo length: one chorus on a slow blues, two on a medium, never more without being waved on. Leave them wanting.
  • The ending: when in doubt, watch the bass player and land the ♭II9–I9 slide with everyone else. Nailing the ending forgives everything that happened before it.

Listening spine (chronological)

  1. Bessie Smith — St. Louis Blues (1925) — phrasing source code
  2. Robert Johnson — King of the Delta Blues Singers — the Delta codex: turnarounds, time, menace
  3. Muddy Waters — The Best of Muddy Waters — the Delta goes electric; the riff as architecture
  4. Howlin' Wolf — Moanin' in the Moonlight — rhythm-section violence, Sumlin's angles
  5. T-Bone Walker — T-Bone Blues — the birth of uptown electric soloing
  6. B.B. King — Live at the Regal — call-and-response, the box, the audience as instrument
  7. Albert King — Born Under a Bad Sign — bending as a complete language
  8. Magic Sam — West Side Soul — the modern small-band blueprint
  9. Stevie Ray Vaughan — Texas Flood — the synthesis, plus the hardest shuffle on record
