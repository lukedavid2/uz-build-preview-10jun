Jazz harmony essentials — the engine room

Six concepts cover ninety percent of what happens in a standard. Take them one at a time — drawn out where drawing helps — and the chord-symbol wall comes down.

Learn this page in three steps
  1. Hear the melt: play Dm7–G7–Cmaj7 slowly and listen for the 7th of each chord falling onto the next 3rd.
  2. Loop the I–VI–ii–V turnaround until you recognise it inside other tunes.
  3. Add one substitution at a time — secondary dominant first, tritone sub second.

1. The ii–V–I — the atom of jazz

In C: Dm7 → G7 → Cmaj7. The roots fall in fifths; inside the chords, the 7th of each resolves down a half-step to the 3rd of the next. That half-step fall is the "melt" you hear:

The minor-key version — Dm7♭5 → G7(♭9) → Cm — is the same engine with darker fuel. Standards are substantially chains of ii–Vs visiting different keys; learn to spot them as units and a sheet of changes becomes three or four sentences instead of thirty symbols.

2. Turnarounds

The jazz turnaround is I–VI–ii–V (C–A7–Dm7–G7): two beats each, landing you back at the top. Every variant is a substitution on that frame: iii–VI–ii–V (Em7 for C), or the half-step "Lady Bird" version I–♭III7–♭VImaj7–♭II7. Loop one for ten minutes and you'll recognise it in a hundred tunes.

3. Secondary dominants

Any chord can be preceded by its own V7 — that's the whole trick. The A7 in a C-major turnaround is "V of ii": it doesn't belong to C, it belongs to the Dm it's aiming at. The chord builder's secondary-dominants row is exactly this device; jazz simply uses it relentlessly.

4. Tritone substitution

Every dominant 7th shares its two crucial notes (the 3rd and 7th — the tritone) with the dominant a flattened fifth away: G7 and D♭7 both contain B and F. Either can resolve to C — and substituting D♭7 turns the ii–V–I bassline into a chromatic slide: D–D♭–C. One substitution rule, applied everywhere, and it's one of the most recognisable sounds in jazz.

5. The passing diminished

Between IV and the I/5 sits the ♯IV diminished (in B♭ blues: E♭7 → E°7 → B♭/F); between I and ii sits ♯I°7. Diminished chords are harmonic stairs — every note wants to step up. You met one in bar 6 of the jazz blues; gospel and Stevie Wonder run on the same stairs.

6. Chord-melody and the walking-bass trick — solo guitar's two pillars

When there's no band, the guitar does everything, and the curriculum is older than it looks: put the melody on the top two strings, harmonise the strong beats with shells underneath (melody note on top, 3rd and 7th below), and leave the weak beats bare. For the full Joe Pass picture, alternate bass note – chord – bass note – chord: a walking quarter-note bass with chord stabs on the off-beats. Both skills are shell voicings in disguise — learn the shells in the comping chapter first and these arrive nearly free.
