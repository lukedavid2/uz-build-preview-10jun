Jazz — a study guide for songwriters

Jazz looks like a wall of chord symbols and sounds like a private language. It isn't. Under the surface, almost everything runs on one engine — the ii–V–I — applied to a handful of song forms that were pop music's own templates for forty years. Seven chapters: the forms, the harmony engine, the comping craft, the soloing language, the transitions, the players, and a study path that doesn't require a conservatory.

The one idea that unlocks everything: jazz harmony is motion, not decoration. The extensions are surface colour; the engine is root movement falling in fifths, and inside it one tiny mechanism — each chord's 7th slipping down a half-step to become the next chord's 3rd. Why songwriters should care

Three reasons, none of them "to sound jazzy". First, voice leading: jazz harmony is a course in making chords melt into each other — the skill behind every sophisticated pop progression from Stevie Wonder to Radiohead. Second, reharmonisation: once you can substitute chords, any tired three-chord chorus can be re-lit without changing the melody. Third, form literacy: AABA is still everywhere — Lennon and McCartney wrote in it constantly — and knowing why the bridge exists makes your bridges better.

If the blues section is the load-bearing wall, this is the plumbing and wiring. Start with forms if you think in songs, or harmony if you think in chords.
