How to study jazz — ten standards and one honest method

The conservatory route drowns beginners in scale theory. The working-musician route is older and better: learn tunes, sing everything, steal vocabulary, play with people. Here is that route, sequenced.

The daily shape — 30 minutes, same skeleton every day
  1. 10 min ear: sing the melody and the roots of this week’s tune with the recording. Non-negotiable, before the instrument comes out.
  2. 15 min hands: the week’s exercises below — shells, guide tones, one phrase.
  3. 5 min play: comp or solo through the whole form over a backing loop, mistakes allowed, no stopping.

The prerequisite

Be able to play a 12-bar blues without thinking, and comp a ii–V–I with shell voicings in two keys. That's the entry ticket — a week's work from the blues section.

The daily ten minutes that beats everything: ear first

Before any instrument practice: pick the tune you're learning and sing — the melody from memory, then the roots of the changes while the recording plays. When you can sing the bass motion of Autumn Leaves▶, you own it in a way no chart can give you. This is the open secret of every serious jazz pedagogy: the instrument only plays what the ear already knows.

The method: one tune at a time, completely

For each tune, in order: (1) learn the melody by ear and memorise it — sing before you play; (2) learn the roots, then shells, until you can comp the form from memory; (3) map the ii–Vs — circle them on paper once, then throw the paper away; (4) solo with chord tones and one transcribed phrase per A section; (5) play it with another human, or at minimum over a looped backing. A tune isn't learned until it survives memory, and ten tunes learned this way beat a hundred read from the page.

The first month, week by week

The method above, sequenced onto real tunes. Each week assumes the daily 30 minutes; each ends with a recorded self-check, because the tape is the only honest teacher.

Week 1 — Now’s the Time

A blues head you can learn in days, so all your attention goes to the new habits.

  • Days 1–2: melody by ear from the record, sung first, then found on the neck. No paper. Loop two bars at a time.
  • Days 3–4: roots on beat 1 through the form, then shells (3rd + 7th) at 80 bpm. Say the chord names aloud.
  • Days 5–7: head → one chorus of chord-tone solo → head, over a loop. Record it on day 7.

You got it when: you can play the head from memory two days after last hearing it, and your solo touches the 3rd of every chord at least once.

Week 2 — Autumn Leaves, part one

The ii–V–I tune. This week is ears and skeleton only — resist soloing.

  • Sing the bass motion with the record until the falling-in-fifths gravity is hummable cold. This is the week’s real work.
  • Map the ii–Vs on paper once — circle them, name the major and the minor engine — then throw the paper away.
  • Shells through the form at 70 bpm, then 90. The Cm7–F7–B♭ and the Am7♭5–D7–Gm engines under your fingers in one position each.

You got it when: you can comp the full 32 bars from memory while saying which engine (major or minor) you’re inside.

Week 3 — Autumn Leaves, part two + your first transcription

  • Guide-tone line through the whole form: 7th falls to 3rd, every change, whole notes only. Then decorate each landing with one neighbour note.
  • Transcribe one two-bar phrase from any Autumn Leaves▶ recording you love (the Cannonball/Miles version is rich ground). Sing it, find it, move it through two keys.
  • Solo one chorus using only chord tones + your phrase, placed where it fits. Record head–solo–head.

You got it when: a listener can hear the changes in your solo with the backing muted.

Week 4 — Blue Bossa + consolidation

  • Blue Bossa▶ by the full method: melody sung then played, roots, shells, ii–V map (spot the key change to D♭ and back — sing the pivot).
  • Alternate days: revisit Now’s the Time▶ and Autumn Leaves▶ from memory. Tunes decay; revision is part of the method.
  • End of week: record all three tunes, head–solo–head, one take each. Keep the recordings — they’re your baseline for month two.

You got it when: three tunes survive from memory in one sitting. That’s a repertoire — small, but real. Continue down the ladder at roughly one tune per fortnight.

Session survival — the protocol

A jazz session runs on a shared script. Knowing it matters more than your vocabulary:

  • The arc is sacred: head (melody) once — or twice for short forms — then solos over the form, then trading, then the head out. Count the form silently through every solo; your turn arrives at a chorus top, never mid-form.
  • Calling a tune is one sentence: "Autumn Leaves▶ in G minor, medium swing, two in and out." Key, feel, arrangement. Then count it in at the tempo you actually want — the count-in is a contract.
  • Comping behind a soloist: you’re in dialogue, not wallpaper. Feed them space (the Charleston and its displacement), drop out entirely for a chorus if there’s a piano — the comp handoff is arranged with a glance.
  • Trading fours: after the solos, soloists alternate four bars each with the drummer. Keep counting — the form never stops underneath.
  • Endings: when the leader taps their head, the head is coming back. Standard close: tag the last four bars three times, then either the Basie "plink" or a held chord with a ritard. Watch the bass player; land together.
  • Ballads are an audition for taste. Half the notes, twice the intention. If you’re not sure, play the melody — nobody has ever objected to the melody.
Before you move on, you should be able to…
  • ☐ Count any form through someone else’s solo and come in correctly at a chorus top.
  • ☐ Call a tune in one sentence and count it in at a usable tempo.
  • ☐ Comp one chorus using only Charleston rhythms, then one chorus of Freddie Green fours.
  • ☐ Tag an ending three times and land the final chord with the bassist.

Months two and three — the intermediate block

Month two — tunes 4–6, comping grows up

  • Repertoire: Summertime▶, Take the A Train▶ and Fly Me to the Moon▶ by the full method, one per ten days. A Train’s II7♯11 and Fly Me’s circle-of-fifths are this month’s harmony lessons in tune form.
  • Comping focus: the comping workout at intermediate tier daily — Charleston displacement and the Red Garland anticipations until they’re reflexes, not decisions.
  • Quota begins: one transcribed phrase per week, sung first, moved through three keys. Log them; this list becomes your voice.
  • First session visit: sit in on one tune you own completely. Comp more than you solo. Debrief in writing.

Month three — tunes 7–8, the solo grows up

  • Repertoire: All of Me▶ (secondary dominants in the wild) and So What▶ (the phrasing exam). Maintain everything previous with a two-tunes-a-day revision rotation.
  • Soloing focus: the soloing workout intermediate tier — enclosures, the motif chorus, guide-tone solos. One constraint chorus daily.
  • Trading: the trading-fours drill from the transitions workout twice a week; the form must survive your own silence.
  • Benchmark: month-end recording of head–solo–head on Autumn Leaves▶, filed next to month one’s. Listen once, write three sentences, move on.

The advanced practice architecture — 60 minutes, ongoing

Past the first ten tunes, progress is architecture plus honesty. The hour below keeps every muscle loaded; rotate the contents weekly so nothing fossilises:

5FreeRecord one chorus of anything; one honest sentence of listen-back notes
  • The repertoire ledger: keep your tunes in three columns — learning, maintaining, performance-ready. A tune may only move right; if one decays, it goes back a column and into the rotation. Target: thirty performance-ready tunes, the working musician’s threshold.
  • Monthly milestones: call and lead a tune at a session; play one melody chorus so well the table stops talking; transpose any A section at sight; add two tunes to the ledger.
  • Quarterly: the benchmark recording, same tune all year. December’s take against March’s is the report card.

Transcription — the non-negotiable

One phrase a week, by ear, from a record. Loop two bars at 75% speed, sing the phrase until you can't get it wrong, find it on the instrument, copy the articulation, then move it through three keys. Twenty weeks of this outperforms any book ever printed. (A note on lead sheets: the Real Book is a map, not the territory — its changes are frequently wrong. Trust the recording.)

Listening spine (one record per era)

  1. Count Basie — The Complete Decca Recordings — swing as a way of life (and Freddie Green's comping)
  2. Charlie Parker — The Savoy & Dial sessions — bebop, the source
  3. Miles Davis — Kind of Blue — modal; the best-selling jazz record for a reason
  4. John Coltrane — Giant Steps — harmony's outer limit (admire first, climb later)
  5. Wes Montgomery — Smokin' at the Half Note — the guitar summit
  6. Herbie Hancock — Maiden Voyage — the modern mainstream's source code
