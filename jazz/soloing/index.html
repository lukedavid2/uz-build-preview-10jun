Jazz soloing — melody under pressure▶ Spotify▶ YouTube

Jazz improvisation has a fearsome reputation it doesn't deserve. The mechanics are learnable and surprisingly few; what takes years is making them sing. Here they are in working order — with the map drawn.

Learn this page in three steps
  1. Land the 3rd of each chord on beat one through a looped ii–V–I — arpeggio tones only.
  2. Sing one transcribed phrase until you cannot get it wrong, then find it on the neck.
  3. Spend a chorus developing ONE motif instead of playing licks.

1. Chord tones first — the unfashionable truth

Before scales: arpeggios. Play just the tones of each chord as it passes, and you already sound like you know the tune. Here is a full ii–V–I's worth of chord tones sitting in one hand position — the soloist's home neighbourhood:

The drill: loop Dm7–G7–Cmaj7 and land the 3rd of each chord on beat one (F, then B, then E). This sounds simplistic and is the actual foundation — bebop lines are decorated arpeggios far more than they are scales.

2. Guide-tone lines — the thread through the maze

Connect the 3rds and 7ths into a single slow melody that snakes through the whole form, moving by step or staying put — the voice-leading diagram IS a guide-tone line. This is the skeleton of every great solo: Lester Young and Charlie Christian are practically guide-tone machines with rhythm. Write one out for a blues, then for Autumn Leaves; improvise around it like decorating a clothesline.

3. Enclosures and approach notes — the bebop finish

Bebop's signature move: don't land on a chord tone, surround it — one fret above, one below, then the target (F–D♯–E to land the 3rd of C). Add the chromatic passing note between scale steps and you have most of the bebop "sound" in two devices. They work because they create tiny dissonances that resolve exactly on the beat.

4. Motif development — what separates solos from exercises

Great soloists play an idea, then argue with it: repeat it higher, invert it, stretch its rhythm, answer it. Sonny Rollins could run ten minutes on three notes. The discipline: improvise a chorus where every phrase audibly derives from your first one. This — not speed — is what audiences hear as "saying something".

5. Rhythm is the actual instrument

More important than note choice: where phrases start. Beginners start on beat one; players start on the and-of-one, beat four, mid-bar. Practise displacing the same lick to five different starting points. And steal the horn players' secret — phrases the length of a breath, with space between them (guitarists never run out of air, which is why we ramble).

6. Sing first — the ear is the instrument behind the instrument

The deepest practice in jazz pedagogy is also the cheapest: sing the line before you play it. If you can't sing it, you're typing, not talking. Sing along with recorded solos until they're memorised, then find them on the neck — the order matters. Ten minutes of this daily outperforms an hour of scale drills for actually hearing changes.

7. The practice loop that compounds

  1. Loop a ii–V–I. Arpeggio tones only, 3rds on the downbeats, until boredom.
  2. Add enclosures on the 3rds.
  3. Write and sing one guide-tone line.
  4. Transcribe ONE two-bar phrase from a record — sing it first, then play it, with the articulation.
  5. Force that phrase into three keys and one real tune. Repeat weekly.
